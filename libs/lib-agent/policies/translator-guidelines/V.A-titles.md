## A. Titles

_[This section is about how to choose and list different title forms. General guidelines for formatting and capitalizing titles may be found at IV.D above.]_

The published translations will include the title as it is provided in the Tibetan source text but with some specific conventions for the collection, which include the use of a "short title" and a "long title" in each of the three languages, plus alternative or other titles that may be useful for anyone trying to identify the text.

The **long title** includes the full title with all the honorific and other non-essential elements from the source text. It is published with the text out of respect for the (mainly Tibetan) convention by which the texts are so identified.

So that there is some consistency among the collection, we suggest rendering _nāma_ (_zhes bya ba_, etc.) in the long title with quotation marks if possible (rather than, e.g., translating it as "called," "known as," etc., or using a colon). This may need to be adjusted to adapt to certain cases.

The titles of tantras will, in general, present more problems in this respect than those of the majority of sūtras. The title pages of published texts supply numerous examples of how these different titles are already used (and some that do not conform and may need revision); if in doubt regarding how to formulate the short and long titles please consult the editors.

For texts whose titles include reference to the main section to which they belong (this is especially the case for the Ratnakūṭa texts) there is no need to include the section details, as the placement of the text within the collection will preserve that information. For subcategories like the Mahāsūtras or the Great Collection (see example below) that do not have their own dedicated section, the corresponding elements in the long title should be included in the same way as honorifics.

In general you should follow these examples:

The Noble Mahāyāna Sūtra "The White Lotus of the Good Dharma"

The Noble Dhāraṇī "Heap of Flowers"

"The Stem Array" Chapter from the Mahāvaipulya Sūtra "A Multitude of Buddhas"

The Noble Mahāyāna Sūtra "The Ratnaketu Dhāraṇī" from the Great Collection

The **short title** is the title with all the standard honorific elements removed, to make the essential elements of the title easier to recognize and to avoid the texts being alphabetically indexed or otherwise listed with a surfeit of repeated honorifics.

Titles for the front-matter title page should be listed as follows:

- The short title in Tibetan or Wylie
- The short title in English
- The short title in Sanskrit
- The long title in Tibetan or Wylie
- The long title in English
- The long title in Sanskrit
  On the front-matter title page, the greatest prominence will be given to the short titles, although the long honorific titles will also appear just below the main titles.

At the beginning of the translation itself, however, i.e., after all the front matter, in the "translation body" section, where the title is given again, the long title including honorifics should be used but (unless there are no honorifics, which may be the case) should be divided into two or more lines with the honorifics separated out, usually above. Some examples of how to arrange these translation body titles are as follows:

The Noble Great Vehicle Sūtra
**The Chapter Teaching the Purification of Boundless Gateways**

The Noble Mahāyāna Sūtra
**The Ratnaketu Dhāraṇī**
from the Great Collection

The Noble Dhāraṇī
**Entering into Nonconceptuality**

The Foundation of All Tantras, the Great Sovereign
**Emergence from Sampuṭa**

Many texts are referred to in commentaries, or by oral tradition, using **alternative titles**, which may be abbreviated forms, nicknames, alternative renderings of their Sanskrit titles, titles used in chapter colophons or the final explicit, or sometimes even alternative names given within the text (e.g., in the case of some sūtras, by the Buddha in the final entrustment passage). As many of these as possible, in the languages in which they are found, should be listed under "alternative titles" and will be included in the database. They may not all be displayed, but will serve to index the text for the purposes of search queries and in some cases philological exploration.

Alternative titles may also be a topic of sufficient significance to justify mention in your introduction, as may instances of such relatively common problems as a mismatch between the Tibetan and Sanskrit titles that requires explanation.

Whenever a Sanskrit title is identified as a back translation (either [1] because it does not appear in the source text, [2] because the title in the source text is not plausible or is uncertain as an authentic Sanskrit title, or [3] because it was back translated by the Tibetan translators), the likelihood of this being the case should always be indicated in the introduction to the translation, along with a brief explanation of one's choices if relevant. Some effort should be made to establish a reasonable Sanskrit title if the Tibetan rendering seems implausible. Some anomalies (such as a wrong word order or a wrong choice of Sanskrit words) could be preserved, but in cases where the back-translated title (or any Sanskrit title) is too corrupt, one should do some basic editing. For example, words that have been misspelled so badly that they have become unintelligible (e.g., sahasra appearing as stasya) should be restored to their intelligible form. If the title contains clusters of misspellings that are typical of Tibetan phonetic renderings of Sanskrit words, the spelling should be "Sanskritized." If it is absolutely certain that the title is a back translation, an asterisk (*) should precede the Sanskrit title. A title back-translated by us because one was not supplied by the Tibetan translators should be included outside the primary list of titles (i.e., as an alternative title).
